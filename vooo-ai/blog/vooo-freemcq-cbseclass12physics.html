Class 12 Physics is widely regarded as one of the most intellectually demanding and simultaneously most rewarding subjects in the entire CBSE curriculum. It forms the scientific backbone of higher education pathways in engineering, medicine, research, and technology. The concepts introduced in Class 12 Physics are not merely academic exercises — they are the fundamental principles that explain how the universe behaves, how modern technology functions, and how the devices and systems we depend on every single day actually work. A strong command over this subject opens doors not just to board exam success but to a lifetime of scientific thinking and problem-solving ability.

Electrostatics, the opening chapter of the Class 12 Physics journey, lays the groundwork for understanding electric charges, electric fields, electric potential, capacitors, and Gauss's Law. These concepts are foundational not only for the board examination but also for competitive entrance exams like JEE Main, JEE Advanced, and NEET. Understanding how charges interact, how electric fields are visualized through field lines, and how capacitors store and release energy provides students with tools that recur across multiple chapters and real-world applications — from touchscreen technology to lightning rods to the functioning of the human nervous system.

Current Electricity builds directly on the concepts of electrostatics, transitioning from static charges to charges in motion. Ohm's Law, Kirchhoff's rules, the Wheatstone bridge, potentiometers, and the internal resistance of cells are among the most frequently tested topics in both board exams and entrance tests. This chapter demands both conceptual clarity and strong numerical problem-solving skills. Students who master circuit analysis in this chapter find themselves significantly better prepared for the electromagnetic chapters that follow, as well as for practical laboratory examinations that form an important component of the CBSE Class 12 assessment structure.

Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism introduces students to one of the most visually intuitive yet conceptually deep areas of physics. From the force experienced by a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field to the working principle of a galvanometer, from the properties of diamagnetic and paramagnetic materials to the Earth's magnetic field — this section bridges classical physics with the modern technologies of electric motors, MRI machines, and magnetic storage devices. Questions from this section appear with high frequency in board papers and are known for testing both direct recall and application-based understanding.

Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents represent the conceptual heart of modern electrical engineering. Faraday's laws, Lenz's Law, self-inductance, mutual inductance, transformers, and AC circuit analysis are topics that directly underpin the generation and distribution of electrical power across the world. Every time a student switches on a light or charges a device, the physics of electromagnetic induction is silently at work. Understanding these principles deeply gives students not just exam marks but genuine insight into the technological infrastructure of modern civilization.

Optics — both Ray Optics and Wave Optics — offers a fascinating exploration of the behavior of light. From the geometry of mirrors and lenses to the wave phenomena of interference, diffraction, and polarization, this section demands both mathematical precision and physical intuition. The Human Eye chapter connects optics directly to biology and medicine, making it particularly relevant for NEET aspirants. Wave Optics, with its elegant explanation of phenomena like Young's Double Slit Experiment, represents one of the most intellectually satisfying sections of the entire Class 12 syllabus.

Modern Physics — covering Dual Nature of Radiation, Atoms, Nuclei, and Semiconductor Devices — brings the Class 12 Physics journey to a thrilling conclusion. The photoelectric effect, Bohr's model of the hydrogen atom, radioactive decay, nuclear fission and fusion, and the working of p-n junction diodes and transistors are all topics of enormous scientific and technological importance. These chapters also carry significant weightage in both CBSE board exams and competitive entrance examinations, making thorough preparation absolutely essential. ⚡ CBSE Class 12 Physics
1SI unit of electric charge is:
A. Volt
B. Ampere
C. Coulomb
D. Farad
Answer: C — Coulomb
The SI unit of electric charge is the Coulomb (C), named after French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb. One coulomb is the charge transported by a current of one ampere in one second (1 C = 1 A·s).
2Which law gives force between two charges?
A. Ohm's Law
B. Coulomb's Law
C. Faraday's Law
D. Gauss's Law
Answer: B — Coulomb's Law
Coulomb's Law states that the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of distance between them: F = kq₁q₂/r².
3The focal length of a concave mirror is:
A. Positive
B. Negative
C. Zero
D. Infinite
Answer: B — Negative
In the standard sign convention, the focal length of a concave mirror is negative (focal point is in front of the mirror). Convex mirrors have positive focal length. This convention: distances measured in direction of incident light are positive.
4Einstein's photoelectric equation is:
A. E = mc²
B. KE = hν − φ
C. E = hν + φ
D. KE = φ − hν
Answer: B — KE = hν − φ
Einstein's photoelectric equation: Maximum Kinetic Energy = hν − φ, where h is Planck's constant, ν is frequency of incident light and φ (phi) is the work function of the metal. This explained the photoelectric effect for which Einstein won the 1921 Nobel Prize.
5Magnetic field lines inside a solenoid are:
A. Diverging
B. Converging
C. Circular
D. Parallel and uniform
Answer: D — Parallel and uniform
Inside a solenoid (a coil of wire carrying current), the magnetic field lines are parallel, uniform and directed along the axis. This uniform field is similar to that inside a bar magnet. The field outside a solenoid is very weak.
6Which phenomenon proves the wave nature of light?
A. Photoelectric effect
B. Compton effect
C. Interference
D. Pair production
Answer: C — Interference
Interference (and diffraction) proves the wave nature of light. The photoelectric effect and Compton effect prove the particle (photon) nature of light. This dual nature — wave and particle — is called wave-particle duality.
7In a p-n junction diode, forward bias means:
A. p-side to negative terminal
B. p-side to positive terminal
C. Both sides to positive
D. No external voltage
Answer: B — p-side to positive terminal
In forward bias, the p-side of the diode is connected to the positive terminal and n-side to the negative terminal of the battery. This reduces the depletion layer and allows current to flow. In reverse bias, the diode blocks current.
8The unit of magnetic flux is:
A. Tesla
B. Weber
C. Gauss
D. Henry
Answer: B — Weber
The SI unit of magnetic flux (Φ) is the Weber (Wb), named after German physicist Wilhelm Eduard Weber. 1 Weber = 1 Tesla × 1 metre². The SI unit of magnetic field (flux density) is the Tesla (T).
9Refractive index of glass with respect to air is 1.5. Speed of light in glass?
A. 3×10⁸ m/s
B. 2×10⁸ m/s
C. 1.5×10⁸ m/s
D. 4.5×10⁸ m/s
Answer: B — 2×10⁸ m/s
Refractive index (n) = Speed of light in vacuum (c) / Speed of light in medium (v). So v = c/n = 3×10⁸/1.5 = 2×10⁸ m/s. Light slows down when entering a denser medium — this causes refraction (bending of light).
10De Broglie wavelength is associated with:
A. Only photons
B. Only electrons
C. All moving particles
D. Stationary particles
Answer: C — All moving particles
De Broglie proposed that all moving particles (not just photons) have an associated wavelength: λ = h/mv, where h is Planck's constant, m is mass and v is velocity. This wave nature of matter was experimentally confirmed by electron diffraction.
