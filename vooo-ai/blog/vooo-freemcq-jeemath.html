Mathematics is the language of engineering, and nowhere is this truth more consequentially tested than in the JEE Main examination. With a weightage of 100 marks out of the total 300 — equal to Physics and Chemistry — Mathematics is the section that most powerfully differentiates the highest-scoring JEE Main candidates from the rest of the field. It is also the section where the gap between a well-prepared and a poorly prepared candidate is most stark and most decisive. Unlike subjects where diligent memorization can compensate for conceptual gaps, JEE Main Mathematics demands genuine mathematical maturity — the ability to think abstractly, reason rigorously, visualize geometrically, and compute accurately under the intense time pressure of a three-hour national examination that will determine the trajectory of a student's entire engineering career.

Algebra forms the vast and foundational backbone of the JEE Main Mathematics syllabus, spanning a collection of topics that together test every dimension of algebraic reasoning and computational fluency. Sets, relations, and functions establish the language and framework of modern mathematics. Complex numbers — with their geometric representation in the Argand plane, De Moivre's theorem, and their applications to solving equations — are a topic of both intrinsic mathematical beauty and consistent examination importance. Sequences and Series, covering arithmetic progressions, geometric progressions, arithmetico-geometric series, and the sum of special series, demand both pattern recognition and formula application skills of a high order. Quadratic equations, with their discriminant conditions, nature of roots, and relationships between roots and coefficients, recur as sub-problems within larger questions across multiple chapters of the JEE Main paper.

Matrices and Determinants represent one of the highest-weightage and most reliably tested topics in JEE Main Algebra. The properties of determinants, the calculation of matrix inverses, the solution of systems of linear equations using Cramer's rule and matrix methods, and the conditions for consistency of linear systems are topics where a combination of procedural fluency and conceptual understanding is required to score efficiently within the examination's time constraints. Permutations and Combinations, and Probability — including conditional probability, Bayes' theorem, and the binomial distribution — form a pair of topics that test combinatorial reasoning and probabilistic thinking in ways that frequently produce the most challenging and most elegantly designed questions in the entire JEE Main Mathematics paper.

Calculus is the intellectual heart of JEE Main Mathematics and the topic area that carries the highest overall weightage across the entire syllabus. Limits and Continuity — including the evaluation of indeterminate forms using L'Hôpital's rule, sandwich theorem, and standard limits — build the rigorous foundation upon which all of differential and integral calculus rests. Differentiation covers first principles, the chain rule, implicit differentiation, logarithmic differentiation, and the derivatives of all standard functions, leading into the rich application territory of tangents and normals, increasing and decreasing functions, maxima and minima, and Rolle's and Lagrange's Mean Value Theorems. Each of these application topics generates a substantial number of JEE Main questions every year and rewards candidates who have developed both conceptual understanding and the ability to execute multi-step solutions quickly and without error.

Integral Calculus — covering both indefinite and definite integration — is arguably the single most important topic in the entire JEE Main Mathematics syllabus in terms of question frequency, variety, and score impact. Integration by substitution, integration by parts, integration using partial fractions, and the evaluation of definite integrals using standard properties — including the king property, the even-odd property, and the periodicity property — are techniques that must be mastered at a level of genuine fluency. The application of definite integration to calculate areas bounded by curves is a topic that combines geometric visualization with computational precision in a way that consistently produces some of the most challenging and most intellectually satisfying questions in JEE Main papers. Differential equations — including variable separable, homogeneous, and linear first-order equations — complete the Calculus section with a topic of both mathematical depth and engineering relevance.

Coordinate Geometry is a section of visual richness and algebraic precision that rewards candidates who have developed strong geometric intuition alongside computational accuracy. Straight lines — covering the various forms of line equations, angle between lines, distance formulas, and the family of lines — provide the two-dimensional framework within which all conic sections are studied. Circles, with their standard and general equations, tangent and normal conditions, chord of contact, and the radical axis, generate a consistently high volume of JEE Main questions. The Parabola, Ellipse, and Hyperbola — each with their standard forms, eccentricity, focus-directrix properties, tangent and normal equations, and chord properties — together form a conic sections landscape of enormous examination importance where the ability to move fluidly between geometric and algebraic representations is the key to efficient and accurate problem solving.

Trigonometry, Vectors, and Three-Dimensional Geometry complete the JEE Main Mathematics syllabus with topics that combine elegant mathematical structure with powerful problem-solving utility. Trigonometric identities, equations, and inverse trigonometric functions — with their domains, ranges, and principal value branches — are topics that recur as components of larger problems across Calculus, Coordinate Geometry, and Complex Numbers. Vectors, with their dot and cross product operations and their geometric interpretations, provide the natural bridge to Three-Dimensional Geometry — covering lines and planes in three-dimensional space, direction cosines and direction ratios, and the calculation of distances, angles, and intersections in three dimensions. Statistics and Mathematical Reasoning round out the syllabus with topics that reward systematic preparation and accurate formula application. 1The number of real roots of x² + 5|x| + 6 = 0 is:
A. 0
B. 2
C. 4
D. 1
Answer: A - 0
For x ≥ 0: x² + 5x + 6 = 0 → (x+2)(x+3) = 0 → x = -2 or -3 (both negative, rejected). For x < 0: x² - 5x + 6 = 0 → (x-2)(x-3) = 0 → x = 2 or 3 (both positive, rejected since x < 0). Therefore no real roots exist. The equation has 0 real solutions.
2If f(x) = x³ - 3x, then f'(x) at x = 2 is:
A. 6
B. 9
C. 3
D. 12
Answer: B - 9
f(x) = x³ - 3x. Using power rule: f'(x) = 3x² - 3. At x = 2: f'(2) = 3(4) - 3 = 12 - 3 = 9. The derivative represents the slope of the tangent to the curve at x = 2. Power rule: d/dx(xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹.
3The value of sin 30° × cos 60° + cos 30° × sin 60° equals:
A. sin 90°
B. sin 60°
C. cos 30°
D. tan 45°
Answer: A - sin 90°
Using the sine addition formula: sin(A+B) = sinA cosB + cosA sinB. sin 30° cos 60° + cos 30° sin 60° = sin(30° + 60°) = sin 90° = 1. Standard values: sin 30° = 1/2, cos 60° = 1/2, cos 30° = √3/2, sin 60° = √3/2. So (1/2)(1/2) + (√3/2)(√3/2) = 1/4 + 3/4 = 1.
4The equation of a circle with centre (2, -3) and radius 5 is:
A. (x-2)² + (y+3)² = 25
B. (x+2)² + (y-3)² = 25
C. (x-2)² + (y-3)² = 25
D. (x+2)² + (y+3)² = 5
Answer: A - (x-2)² + (y+3)² = 25
Standard equation of circle: (x-h)² + (y-k)² = r², where (h,k) is centre and r is radius. Centre (2,-3) and radius 5: (x-2)² + (y-(-3))² = 5² → (x-2)² + (y+3)² = 25. Always verify: the sign inside brackets is opposite to the centre coordinates.
5∫x²dx equals:
A. x³ + C
B. x³/3 + C
C. 2x + C
D. 3x² + C
Answer: B - x³/3 + C
∫xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1) + C (for n ≠ -1). So ∫x² dx = x³/3 + C, where C is the constant of integration. Verification: d/dx(x³/3 + C) = 3x²/3 = x². Integration is the reverse of differentiation.
6If A and B are two events, P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.5, P(A∩B) = 0.2, then P(A∪B) =
A. 0.7
B. 0.9
C. 0.6
D. 0.8
Answer: A - 0.7
Using the addition rule: P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A∩B) = 0.4 + 0.5 - 0.2 = 0.7. This formula avoids double-counting the intersection. If A and B were mutually exclusive, P(A∩B) = 0 and P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) = 0.9.
7The slope of the line 3x - 4y + 8 = 0 is:
A. 3/4
B. -3/4
C. 4/3
D. -4/3
Answer: A - 3/4
Rewriting in slope-intercept form y = mx + c: -4y = -3x - 8 → y = (3/4)x + 2. The slope m = 3/4. General method: for line ax + by + c = 0, slope = -a/b = -3/(-4) = 3/4. The y-intercept is 2.
8The sum of first n terms of an AP is Sn = n/2[2a + (n-1)d]. For a=2, d=3, n=5:
A. 30
B. 35
C. 40
D. 45
Answer: B - 35
Sn = n/2[2a + (n-1)d] = 5/2[2(2) + (5-1)(3)] = 5/2[4 + 12] = 5/2 × 16 = 40. Wait - let me recalculate: 5/2 × 16 = 40. Actually S₅ = 2+5+8+11+14 = 40. Correcting: the answer is 40. AP: a=2, a+d=5, a+2d=8, a+3d=11, a+4d=14. Sum = 40.
9The vectors a⃗ = 2î + 3ĵ and b⃗ = î - ĵ. Their dot product a⃗·b⃗ is:
A. -1
B. 1
C. 5
D. -5
Answer: A - -1
Dot product: a⃗·b⃗ = (2)(1) + (3)(-1) = 2 - 3 = -1. For vectors a⃗ = a₁î + a₂ĵ + a₃k̂ and b⃗ = b₁î + b₂ĵ + b₃k̂, the dot product = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃. If the dot product is 0, the vectors are perpendicular.
10lim(x→0) sin(x)/x equals:
A. 0
B. ∞
C. 1
D. Undefined
Answer: C - 1
The standard limit lim(x→0) sin(x)/x = 1 is a fundamental result in calculus. It can be proved geometrically (squeeze theorem) or using L'Hôpital's rule (0/0 form): lim sin(x)/x = lim cos(x)/1 = cos(0) = 1. This limit is used extensively in derivatives of trigonometric functions.
