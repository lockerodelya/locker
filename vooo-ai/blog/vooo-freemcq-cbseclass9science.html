CBSE Class 9 Science

CBSE Class 9 Science MCQ Practice

Essential questions covering Matter, Atoms and Molecules, Cell Biology, Motion, Force and Gravitation

These essential MCQ questions are selected from the most important topics in CBSE Class 9 Science as per the latest NCERT syllabus. Sections include Matter in Our Surroundings, Atoms and Molecules, Cell — The Fundamental Unit of Life, Motion, Force and Laws of Motion, and Gravitation.

CBSE Class 9 Science is widely regarded as one of the most important and transformative years in a student's scientific education, marking the transition from the broadly exploratory science of the middle school years to the rigorous, concept-driven, and mathematically enriched science that will define Class 10, Class 11, and Class 12. The NCERT syllabus for Class 9 Science introduces students to the foundational principles of Physics, Chemistry, and Biology at a level of depth and precision that demands genuine conceptual understanding rather than surface-level memorisation. A student who builds a strong and confident command of Class 9 Science will find the challenges of higher secondary science significantly more manageable and rewarding.

Matter in Our Surroundings opens the Class 9 Chemistry journey by examining the physical nature of matter — the substance of everything in the universe — through the lens of its states, properties, and the conditions under which it changes from one state to another. Students learn about the characteristics of solids, liquids, and gases in terms of the arrangement, spacing, and movement of their particles, and they explore the processes of evaporation, condensation, melting, freezing, sublimation, and boiling with careful attention to the energy changes involved in each process. MCQs from this chapter test understanding of the factors affecting evaporation, the concept of latent heat, the conditions required for different state changes, the difference between boiling and evaporation, and the everyday applications of these processes in phenomena such as sweating, the formation of dew, and the working of a pressure cooker.

Atoms and Molecules takes students deeper into the structure of matter by introducing the fundamental building blocks of all chemical substances — atoms and molecules — and developing the quantitative language of Chemistry through the concepts of atomic mass, molecular mass, and the mole. Students learn about Dalton's Atomic Theory, the laws of chemical combination including the Law of Conservation of Mass and the Law of Constant Proportions, the writing of chemical formulae, and the calculation of molar mass and the number of particles in a given sample of a substance. MCQs from this chapter test the ability to write and interpret chemical formulae, calculate molecular masses, apply the laws of chemical combination, understand the concept of the mole as the chemist's counting unit, and perform basic stoichiometric calculations that form the quantitative backbone of all future Chemistry study.

Structure of the Atom builds upon the introduction to atoms by tracing the historical development of our understanding of atomic structure from Dalton's solid sphere model through Thomson's plum pudding model, Rutherford's nuclear model, and Bohr's planetary model to the modern quantum mechanical description. Students learn about the discovery of electrons, protons, and neutrons, the concept of atomic number and mass number, the arrangement of electrons in shells and the rules governing electronic configuration, and the significance of valence electrons in determining the chemical behaviour of elements. MCQs from this chapter test knowledge of the key experiments and scientists in the history of atomic theory, the calculation of atomic number, mass number, and the number of subatomic particles from given data, the electronic configuration of the first eighteen elements, and the concept of isotopes and isobars.

Cell — The Fundamental Unit of Life introduces students to the microscopic world of the cell, the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms, and develops an understanding of how the extraordinary complexity of life is organised at the cellular level. Students learn about the discovery of the cell by Robert Hooke, the development of Cell Theory, the structure and functions of the key organelles of both plant and animal cells including the nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and vacuoles, and the important structural differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. MCQs from this chapter test knowledge of the functions of each organelle, the differences between plant and animal cells, the concept of osmosis and its importance in cellular processes, and the structural and functional differences between prokaryotic bacteria and the more complex eukaryotic cells of plants, animals, and fungi.

Motion is the chapter that introduces students to the precise, quantitative language of kinematics — the science of describing how objects move — and equips them with the mathematical tools needed to analyse motion in a rigorous and systematic way. Students learn the definitions of distance and displacement, speed and velocity, and acceleration, and they develop the ability to represent motion graphically using distance-time and velocity-time graphs from which key information about the nature and rate of motion can be extracted. The three equations of uniformly accelerated motion — relating initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time, and displacement — are derived and applied to a wide range of numerical problems. MCQs from this chapter test the ability to distinguish between scalar and vector quantities, interpret motion graphs, apply the equations of motion to solve numerical problems, and understand the concept of uniform circular motion and the acceleration associated with it.

Force and Laws of Motion builds upon the study of motion by introducing the concept of force as the cause of changes in the state of motion of objects, and developing Newton's three Laws of Motion that form the cornerstone of classical mechanics. Students learn about Newton's First Law and the concept of inertia, Newton's Second Law and the quantitative relationship between force, mass, and acceleration expressed in the equation F equals ma, and Newton's Third Law and the principle that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. The concept of momentum and the Law of Conservation of Momentum are also introduced, with applications to everyday phenomena such as the recoil of a gun, the propulsion of rockets, and the physics of collisions. MCQs from this chapter test understanding of Newton's three laws, the calculation of force, acceleration, and momentum in numerical problems, and the application of the Law of Conservation of Momentum to practical situations.

Gravitation introduces students to one of the four fundamental forces of nature — gravity — and develops their understanding of how this universal attractive force governs the motion of planets, the falling of objects, and the weight we experience every day. Students learn about Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation, the concept of gravitational acceleration and its value on Earth, the difference between mass and weight, the concept of free fall, and the behaviour of objects in the absence of gravity. The chapter also covers the phenomena of buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle, which explain why objects float or sink in fluids and have important practical applications in the design of ships, submarines, and hot air balloons. MCQs from this chapter test the ability to apply Newton's Law of Gravitation to calculate gravitational force, understand the variation of gravitational acceleration with altitude and depth, distinguish between mass and weight, and apply Archimedes' Principle to problems involving buoyancy and relative density.

1Which of the following is a physical change?
A. Burning of wood
B. Rusting of iron
C. Melting of ice
D. Digestion of food
Answer: C — Melting of ice
A physical change is reversible and does not produce a new substance. Melting of ice (H₂O solid → H₂O liquid) is a physical change — the chemical composition remains the same. Burning of wood, rusting of iron, and digestion of food are chemical changes because new substances with different properties are formed and the changes are largely irreversible.
2The atomic number of an element is defined as the number of:
A. Neutrons in the nucleus
B. Protons in the nucleus
C. Electrons in outer shell
D. Nucleons in the nucleus
Answer: B — Protons in the nucleus
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons in the nucleus. In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons. Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. Number of neutrons = A - Z. For example, Carbon has Z = 6 (6 protons) and A = 12 (6 protons + 6 neutrons). Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
3An object travels 20 m in 4 seconds. What is its speed?
A. 80 m/s
B. 5 m/s
C. 0.2 m/s
D. 24 m/s
Answer: B — 5 m/s
Speed = Distance / Time = 20 m / 4 s = 5 m/s. Speed is a scalar quantity (only magnitude, no direction). Velocity is a vector quantity (magnitude + direction). Average speed = total distance / total time. If the object's direction is also specified, the 5 m/s becomes velocity. The SI unit of speed is metres per second (m/s).
4Newton's first law of motion is also called the law of:
A. Inertia
B. Momentum
C. Action-Reaction
D. Gravitation
Answer: A — Inertia
Newton's first law states that a body at rest stays at rest and a body in motion stays in motion with the same speed and direction unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force. This tendency is called inertia. More massive objects have greater inertia. Example: passengers jerk forward when a bus brakes suddenly — they were in motion and inertia keeps them moving forward.
5The value of acceleration due to gravity (g) on Earth's surface is approximately:
A. 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ m/s²
B. 9.8 m/s²
C. 9.8 m/s²
D. 3.8 m/s²
Answer: B — 9.8 m/s²
The acceleration due to gravity on Earth's surface is g = 9.8 m/s² (often approximated as 10 m/s²). It is directed towards the centre of the Earth. Weight = mass × g. On the Moon, g = 1.63 m/s² (about 1/6th of Earth). The universal gravitational constant G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg² is different from g. g varies slightly with altitude and latitude.
6Which organelle controls all activities of the cell?
A. Mitochondria
B. Cell membrane
C. Nucleus
D. Ribosome
Answer: C — Nucleus
The nucleus is the control centre of the cell. It contains chromosomes made of DNA that carry genetic information and direct all cell activities including growth, metabolism, and reproduction. It is surrounded by a nuclear membrane with pores. Prokaryotic cells (like bacteria) lack a membrane-bound nucleus. The nucleolus inside the nucleus is the site of ribosome synthesis.
7Which of the following has the highest kinetic energy among particles?
A. Solid particles
B. Liquid particles
C. Gas particles
D. All have equal KE
Answer: C — Gas particles
Gas particles have the highest kinetic energy. In solids, particles vibrate about fixed positions. In liquids, particles can flow and have more energy than solids. In gases, particles move freely and randomly with maximum kinetic energy and maximum intermolecular distances. As temperature increases, kinetic energy of particles increases. At absolute zero (0 K or -273°C), particle movement theoretically stops.
8The momentum of an object of mass 2 kg moving at 5 m/s is:
A. 2.5 kg m/s
B. 7 kg m/s
C. 10 kg m/s
D. 3 kg m/s
Answer: C — 10 kg m/s
Momentum (p) = mass × velocity = 2 kg × 5 m/s = 10 kg m/s. Momentum is a vector quantity. Newton's second law: Force = rate of change of momentum = m × a. The law of conservation of momentum states that the total momentum of an isolated system remains constant. This explains why a gun recoils when fired — the bullet and gun have equal and opposite momenta.
9Which type of tissue is responsible for movement in animals?
A. Epithelial tissue
B. Connective tissue
C. Muscular tissue
D. Nervous tissue
Answer: C — Muscular tissue
Muscular tissue contains cells (muscle fibres) that can contract and relax to produce movement. There are three types: striated/skeletal (voluntary, attached to bones), smooth/unstriated (involuntary, in internal organs), and cardiac (involuntary, in heart). Nervous tissue transmits signals. Epithelial tissue covers body surfaces. Connective tissue provides support and links organs.
10By which process does a solid directly convert into gas without passing through the liquid state?
A. Evaporation
B. Condensation
C. Sublimation
D. Vaporisation
Answer: C — Sublimation
Sublimation is the process by which a solid directly converts to gas (vapour) without going through the liquid phase. Examples: dry ice (solid CO₂), camphor, iodine, and naphthalene balls. The reverse process (gas → solid directly) is called deposition or desublimation. Sublimation is used in freeze-drying food and in purifying substances. Evaporation is liquid → gas; condensation is gas → liquid.
