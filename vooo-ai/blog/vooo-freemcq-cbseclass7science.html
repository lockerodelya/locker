CBSE Class 7 Science

CBSE Class 7 Science MCQ Practice

Essential NCERT-based questions covering Nutrition, Heat, Motion and Living World

These essential MCQ questions are selected from the most important topics in CBSE Class 7 Science examinations. Sections include Nutrition in Plants and Animals, Heat, Acids and Bases, Weather, Motion and Time, and Forests as per NCERT syllabus.

CBSE Class 7 Science represents a significant step forward in a student's scientific education, building confidently upon the foundational concepts introduced in Class 6 and introducing a richer, more detailed, and more analytically demanding understanding of the natural world. The NCERT syllabus for Class 7 Science has been carefully structured to deepen a student's engagement with Biology, Physics, and Chemistry simultaneously, ensuring that learners develop a broad and balanced scientific perspective at this crucial stage of their schooling. The habits of observation, experimentation, and logical reasoning that students cultivate in Class 7 Science will serve them powerfully throughout their academic careers and beyond.

Nutrition in Plants is one of the most important and conceptually rich chapters in the Class 7 Science syllabus, introducing students to the extraordinary process by which green plants manufacture their own food using sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide in the process known as photosynthesis. Students learn about the role of chlorophyll, the significance of stomata, the difference between autotrophs and heterotrophs, and the various modes of nutrition found in the plant kingdom including parasitic, saprophytic, and symbiotic nutrition. MCQs from this chapter test understanding of the photosynthesis equation, the raw materials and products of the process, the functions of different parts of a leaf, and the fascinating adaptations of insectivorous plants such as the Venus flytrap and the pitcher plant that have evolved to supplement their nutrition in nutrient-poor environments.

Nutrition in Animals takes the study of nutrition further by examining how different animals, including human beings, obtain and process their food to extract the energy and nutrients they need to survive, grow, and reproduce. Students explore the human digestive system in detail, learning about the journey of food from ingestion through the mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine to its eventual absorption and elimination. The chapter also covers the nutrition of simpler organisms such as Amoeba, which engulfs food through a process called phagocytosis, and introduces the concept of ruminants and the special digestive adaptations that allow grass-eating animals to break down cellulose. MCQs from this chapter test knowledge of the organs of the digestive system, the enzymes and digestive juices involved in digestion, the sites of absorption of different nutrients, and the comparative nutrition of various animal groups.

Heat is a chapter that connects the familiar experience of warmth and cold with the precise scientific concepts of temperature, heat transfer, and thermal expansion. Students learn the important distinction between heat and temperature, understand how thermometers work and how to read them correctly, and explore the three modes of heat transfer — conduction, convection, and radiation — with examples drawn from everyday life. MCQs from this chapter test understanding of the difference between heat and temperature, the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales, the concept of conductors and insulators of heat, the mechanism of land and sea breezes as examples of convection, and the practical applications of radiation in everyday objects such as thermos flasks and solar cookers.

Acids and Bases introduces students to one of the most fundamental and practically important distinctions in Chemistry — the difference between acidic, basic, and neutral substances — and connects this distinction to the everyday world of food, cleaning products, medicines, and natural phenomena. Students learn to use indicators such as litmus paper and turmeric to identify acids and bases, understand the concept of neutralisation and its applications in agriculture and medicine, and begin to appreciate how chemical reactions between acids and bases produce salts and water. MCQs from this chapter test knowledge of common acids and bases found in everyday life, the colours of indicators in acidic and basic solutions, the pH scale, the concept of neutralisation, and the importance of maintaining the correct acid-base balance in soil for healthy plant growth.

Weather, Climate and Adaptations of Animals to Climate is a chapter that broadens a student's scientific horizon to encompass the atmosphere, the dynamic weather systems that shape daily life, and the remarkable ways in which animals have evolved to survive in different climatic conditions around the world. Students learn about the elements of weather including temperature, humidity, rainfall, wind speed, and cloud cover, and begin to understand the difference between weather and climate. They explore the climate and animal adaptations of polar regions and tropical rainforests, learning about the insulating blubber of polar bears, the migration of Siberian cranes, the camouflage of tropical animals, and the extraordinary biodiversity of rainforest ecosystems. MCQs from this chapter test understanding of weather instruments, climate zones, the monsoon, and the specific adaptations that enable animals to thrive in extreme environments.

Motion and Time introduces students to the precise scientific measurement and description of motion, building on the basic concepts of movement introduced in Class 6 and adding the important dimension of time to create a more complete and quantitative picture of how objects move. Students learn about uniform and non-uniform motion, the concept of speed and how to calculate it, the use of distance-time graphs to represent and interpret motion, and the importance of standard units of time in scientific measurement. MCQs from this chapter test the ability to calculate speed from distance and time data, interpret distance-time graphs, distinguish between uniform and non-uniform motion, and understand the historical development of time-measuring devices from sundials and water clocks to modern digital instruments.

Forests: Our Lifeline is a chapter of profound ecological and social importance, helping students understand that forests are not merely collections of trees but complex, interdependent ecosystems that sustain life on Earth in countless ways. Students explore the layers of a forest ecosystem, the roles of producers, consumers, and decomposers in maintaining ecological balance, the importance of forests in regulating climate, purifying water, preventing soil erosion, and providing habitat for biodiversity. MCQs from this chapter test knowledge of the components of a forest ecosystem, the water cycle and its relationship to forests, the concept of a food web, the causes and consequences of deforestation, and the importance of conservation efforts in protecting India's and the world's remaining forest cover.

1Plants that make their own food are called:
A. Parasites
B. Saprophytes
C. Autotrophs
D. Heterotrophs
Answer: C — Autotrophs
Autotrophs (or autotrophic organisms) are those that produce their own food using sunlight, water and carbon dioxide through photosynthesis. Plants, algae and some bacteria are autotrophs. Animals are heterotrophs — they depend on others for food.
2Which instrument measures temperature?
A. Barometer
B. Thermometer
C. Hygrometer
D. Anemometer
Answer: B — Thermometer
A thermometer measures temperature. A barometer measures atmospheric pressure, a hygrometer measures humidity, and an anemometer measures wind speed. Clinical thermometers use mercury or digital sensors to measure body temperature.
3Litmus is a natural indicator obtained from:
A. Flowers
B. Lichens
C. Leaves
D. Roots
Answer: B — Lichens
Litmus is a natural acid-base indicator extracted from lichens (a symbiotic organism of fungi and algae). Red litmus turns blue in bases; blue litmus turns red in acids. Neutral solutions do not change the colour of litmus.
4The SI unit of speed is:
A. km/h
B. m/min
C. m/s
D. cm/s
Answer: C — m/s
The SI unit of speed is metres per second (m/s). Speed = Distance / Time. While km/h is commonly used in everyday life, m/s is the standard scientific unit. 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h.
5Which nutrient provides energy to our body?
A. Vitamins
B. Minerals
C. Carbohydrates
D. Water
Answer: C — Carbohydrates
Carbohydrates are the primary source of energy for the human body. They are broken down into glucose, which cells use for energy. Proteins build and repair tissues, vitamins and minerals regulate body functions, and water aids all metabolic processes.
6Land breeze blows from:
A. Sea to land during day
B. Land to sea during night
C. Sea to land during night
D. Land to sea during day
Answer: B — Land to sea during night
Land breeze blows from land to sea during the night, because land cools faster than sea after sunset, making the air over land cooler and denser. Sea breeze blows from sea to land during the day as land heats faster.
7Woollen clothes keep us warm because wool:
A. Reflects heat
B. Is a bad conductor of heat
C. Absorbs cold
D. Generates heat
Answer: B — Is a bad conductor of heat
Wool is a bad conductor (poor conductor) of heat, meaning it does not allow heat to pass through easily. It traps air between its fibres, which also acts as an insulator. This prevents body heat from escaping, keeping us warm.
8Forests are called "lungs of the Earth" because they:
A. Breathe like animals
B. Produce large amounts of oxygen
C. Absorb water
D. Provide shelter
Answer: B — Produce large amounts of oxygen
Forests are called the lungs of the Earth because they absorb carbon dioxide and release large amounts of oxygen through photosynthesis. They also regulate climate, maintain the water cycle and support biodiversity.
9Which organ in the human body produces bile?
A. Stomach
B. Pancreas
C. Liver
D. Small intestine
Answer: C — Liver
Bile is produced by the liver and stored in the gall bladder. It is released into the small intestine to help digest fats by breaking large fat globules into smaller droplets — a process called emulsification.
10A simple pendulum completes one oscillation in 2 seconds. Its time period is:
A. 1 second
B. 2 seconds
C. 4 seconds
D. 0.5 seconds
Answer: B — 2 seconds
The time period of a pendulum is the time taken to complete one full oscillation (swing back and forth). If one oscillation takes 2 seconds, the time period = 2 seconds. Frequency = 1/Time Period = 0.5 Hz.
